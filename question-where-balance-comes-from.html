Question

Where does the balance come from?

How the NWSL, with a salary cap and no promotion or relegation, differs from European women's leagues.

Executive summary

Women's top flights are far less balanced than the men's leagues in the same countries. Across England, Spain, France and Germany, the gap between the first- and last-place team averages about 5.3 goals per game, against 2.8 in the men's leagues (1.9 once each men's league is cut to the same size). The NWSL is the exception: its gap of 2.0 is close to MLS's 1.9, and seven different teams finished first in nine seasons.

That balance is not the same as strength. It comes largely from a hard salary cap, which keeps teams even by limiting what any one club can spend, and so limits how much top talent any club can hold. The world's best players show it: the NWSL had 29 of the Guardian's top 100 in 2019 but 18 in 2025, spread across 16 teams, while the WSL rose to 42 from 12. Meanwhile the elite is concentrating in a few clubs. In 2025, Chelsea, Arsenal, Barcelona and Lyon held 51 of the top 100, and Barcelona players have won six of the eight Women's Ballon d'Ors.

The takeaway: the NWSL's salary cap has made its league more equal, not stronger. It holds fewer of the world's top 100 players than it did before 2020, while the WSL's share has more than doubled since 2019, a pattern consistent with the cap holding back the league's talent. Europe has the opposite problem: plenty of talent, packed into a handful of clubs. Neither model works on its own. Clubs that want to compete should invest in youth the way Barcelona has, building a dominant side around academy players such as Putellas, Bonmatí and Pina, and owners should see spending as the way the best clubs grow. Leagues should accept that, in a game this young, the best-funded clubs will lead for a while. The real choice is between more equal games now at the cost of a weaker league, as in the NWSL, or some short-term inequality that rewards the investors growing the game. The better path is to raise the floor through minimum salaries, shared revenue and youth investment, rather than cap the ceiling.

Where the world's best players play

These visualizations draw on two rankings. The first is the Guardian's annual list of the 100 best women's footballers in the world, chosen by a panel of experts (published by the Guardian since 2018, and by The Offside Rule in 2016 and 2017). The charts count where those players play: which leagues and which clubs hold the top talent. The second is the Women's Ballon d'Or, another established ranking, which names one winner each year; winning it is considered one of the biggest individual accomplishments in the sport.

† Alexia Putellas is counted at London City Lionesses, the club she was at when she won, which follows the same rule as the rest of this page. She joined London City in summer 2026, a few months before the ceremony, and the award covers the 2025–26 season she played with Barcelona, winning Liga F, the Copa de la Reina, the Supercopa, and the Champions League. In her acceptance speech, she heavily credits Barcelona for the award. The award lists both clubs.

Beyond club representation, the winners themselves tell a story. Barcelona players won six of the eight Women's Ballon d'Ors awarded so far (the official listing names both Barcelona and London City for Putellas in 2026, so she counts for both here), but the more striking pattern is who won them: two different winners in 2018 and 2019, then only Aitana Bonmatí and Alexia Putellas since 2021. On its own that looks like extreme concentration, but the men's award went through the same thing: from 2008 to 2017, ten Ballon d'Ors in a row went to either Cristiano Ronaldo or Lionel Messi, five each (Ballon d'Or, n.d.-b). This is where the club side matters. Messi and Ronaldo spent most of that run at rival clubs, Barcelona and Real Madrid, while Bonmatí and Putellas won theirs as teammates at the same club, Barcelona.
