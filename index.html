Talent Dispersion

How unevenly is talent spread across women's football? This project compares final league tables from five women's and five men's top flights to measure the gap between the best and worst teams, and what it means for growing the game.

Key takeaways

Findings

  1. Results in women's leagues are far more uneven than in the men's leagues. Across the four European leagues, the gap between the first- and last-place team averages about 5.3 goals per game for women, against 2.8 for men, and 1.9 once each men's league is cut to the size of the women's league.
  2. The same clubs win almost every year. Over the last decade (2016–17 to 2025–26), Lyon won 9 of 10 French titles, Chelsea won 8 English titles, and Barcelona won 7 Spanish titles, all in a row. In Germany, Bayern and Wolfsburg took first and second place every single season.
  3. The NWSL is the exception. Its gap (2.0 goals per game) is close to MLS's (1.9), and 7 different teams finished first in the 9 seasons in the data.

Takeaways

  1. Women's football has a concentration problem: results, and very likely talent, sit in a handful of clubs in each country.
  2. The NWSL's balance comes largely from its hard salary cap, so it is partly by design. Capping what every team can spend keeps teams even, but it also puts a ceiling on how much top talent any one team can afford, and it makes it harder to keep stars who are offered more in Europe. Whether the cap is good or bad for the league is still an open question, but its balance should not be read as proof of a deep talent pool.
  3. The concentration is not fixing itself. A different champion in one season, such as Manchester City in England in 2025–26, is not yet a trend. Spreading talent more evenly will take deliberate choices by leagues, clubs and owners.
