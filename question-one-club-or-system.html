Question

Is it one club or the whole system?

What happens to the numbers when the dominant teams, such as FC Barcelona, are removed.

Executive summary

Within each league, the answer looks like one club: Barcelona in Spain, Lyon in France, Chelsea in England, and Wolfsburg and Bayern in Germany. Zoomed out to Europe, the picture is worse. Clubs from 50 associations (each country counts as one, so England, home of the WSL, is a single association) entered the 2025/26 Champions League, yet over the last ten seasons only eight clubs reached a semi-final, filling all 40 places, and Barcelona made nine of the ten. The men's competition over the same decade had 20 different semi-finalists, and its most frequent one, Real Madrid, made six. Women's football does not need its best clubs to get worse; it needs more clubs able to challenge them. A few more competitive teams in each league would bring some surprise back to the women's game and push the top sides harder. London City Lionesses, near the top of the WSL this season, are an early sign of what that could look like.

Champions League performance

If a few clubs really are too good for everyone else, the Champions League is where it should show. It is the one competition where the best teams from different leagues play each other, and it is open to clubs from all of UEFA's 55 member associations. In 2025/26, 74 teams from 50 different associations entered (UEFA, 2025). Yet very few of them ever get far. The chart below counts which clubs reached each stage over the last ten seasons.

System translation

The same eight clubs that dominate the Champions League also hold most of the game's elite talent. In every year from 2016 to 2025, Barcelona, Lyon, PSG, Chelsea, Arsenal, Manchester City, Wolfsburg and Bayern Munich held between 49 and 69 of the world's top 100 players, and 80–92% of the top-100 players in the four European leagues. Those leagues have roughly 50 top-flight clubs between them, so about a sixth of the clubs hold almost all of the elite talent.

There is a second tier, but it stops at the quarter-finals. Sixteen other clubs reached the last eight over the decade, including Real Madrid, Rosengård, Slavia Praha, Juventus, Roma, Ajax and Benfica, and none of them went further. The problem is not that no other clubs exist; it is the jump from good to elite. The clubs that could be built up into challengers are already showing up in Europe.

The NWSL does not appear in this section because NWSL clubs do not play in the Champions League. That is not a judgment on the league. This page looks at the leagues with the biggest talent gaps, which the NWSL is not, and the Champions League is the one tournament where those leagues' clubs meet directly, which makes it the right measure here.

What would change it

Two different problems need two different fixes. The gap between the best and worst team is similar in all four European leagues, but it comes from different places. In Spain and France it comes mostly from the top: the champion wins by close to four goals a game, while the bottom teams are no worse than elsewhere. Those leagues need a real challenger, which means investment in a second club such as Real Madrid in Spain or PSG in France. In Germany the gap comes mostly from the bottom: its last-place teams lose by almost three goals a game, the worst of the four. Germany needs its floor raised, with better minimum salaries, facilities and squads for its weakest clubs. A single league-wide fix, like a salary cap, cannot solve both problems.

The top clubs have no reason to change this on their own. Dominating at home does not hold these clubs back in Europe; if anything it goes with winning there. The two leagues with the most dominant champions, Spain and France, produced 15 of the last 20 Champions League finalists and 9 of the 10 winners. A club that wins its league by four goals a game, and then wins in Europe, gains nothing from a more competitive league. If the gap is going to close, the push has to come from the leagues and from UEFA, not from the clubs at the top.

Back the second tier. Sixteen other clubs reached the Champions League quarter-finals over the decade, so the next layer of clubs already exists and already competes in Europe. They are the obvious place to invest. Sharing more of the money from European competition across each league, rather than leaving it with the clubs that win it, would go directly to the clubs closest to becoming challengers, and would give each league more than one or two teams that can compete at the top.
